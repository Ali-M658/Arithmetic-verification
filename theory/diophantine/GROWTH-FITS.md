# Growth fits for the note (moved from Section 6.3 of paper/arith/note.tex)

Descriptive fits of the exact counts of the enumeration (Table 3 of the note), produced by
`exponent_fits.py` (output `data/exponent_fits.txt`) and `growth_fits.py`. They describe the range
$S\le6000$ only and bear on Question E of the note in neither direction. The text below is the
former Section 6.3 of the note, verbatim (LaTeX notation; labels refer to the note).

The local exponent in Table~\ref{tab:density} is $1.99$ at $X=500$ and falls to $1.58$ at $X=6000$; at $X=5400$, where $\N(5400)=123\,559$, it is $1.60$. Its larger values below $X=500$ rest on few pairs. The ratio $\N(X)/X^2$ falls from $0.0094$ at $X=400$ to $0.0040$ at $X=6000$, so a quadratic law is not supported by the data. The quantity of Question~\ref{q:intro} is $A(X)$. The enumerations give $A(500)=1328$, $A(1000)=4141$, $A(2000)=12\,512$, $A(3000)=23\,207$, $A(4000)=35\,567$, $A(4800)=46\,254$, $A(5400)=54\,897$ and $A(6000)=63\,876$, with local exponents $\log_2(A(X)/A(X/2))$ of $1.64$, $1.60$, $1.51$, $1.48$, $1.47$ and $1.46$ at $X=1000$, $2000$, $4000$, $4800$, $5400$ and $6000$, below those of $\N$ and also falling.

Around any smooth trend the per-sum counts fluctuate with the divisors of $s$, since by Lemma~\ref{lem:copies} $n(s)=\sum_{d\mid s}n_{\rm prim}(s/d)$, with $n_{\rm prim}(m)$ the number of primitive pairs at sum $m$. As descriptive summaries of the counts on $600\le s\le4800$ we fit two-parameter laws by Poisson maximum likelihood. The enumeration is complete, and the Poisson model is a device for weighting the counts, not a model of them; the counts are overdispersed relative to it, with deviance $6.3$ times its number of degrees of freedom. The law $n(s)=c(\log s)^q$ gives $q\approx4.5$ for all pairs and $q\approx3.5$ for primitive pairs. A least-squares fit of $\log(\N(S)-\N(599))$ against $\log\sum_{s=600}^{S}c(\log s)^{q}$ gives $4.6$ and $3.6$, but it is not an independent estimate, since it fits the partial sums of the same counts. The power law $n(s)=cs^{\beta}$ gives $\beta\approx0.59$ for all pairs, that is, $\N(X)$ growing like $X^{1.59}$ on this range. None of these numbers is stable. On the windows of width $2400$ from $[600,3000]$ to $[3600,6000]$, in steps of $600$, $q$ falls from $4.6$ to $4.2$ for all pairs and, not monotonically, from $3.7$ to $3.1$ for primitive pairs, and $\beta$ falls from $0.62$ to $0.50$. On the sums with $\gcd(s,6)=1$, the odd sums, the even sums and the multiples of $6$ in $[600,4800]$, $q$ is $4.1$, $4.3$, $4.6$ and $5.0$, because $n(s)$ is a divisor sum.

Scaling accounts for the difference of about one between all and primitive pairs: if the primitive pairs with sum at most $y$ number about $y(\log y)^{a}$, then the sum $\N(X)=\sum_D\lfloor X/S_D\rfloor$ of Lemma~\ref{lem:copies} is about $X(\log X)^{a+1}/(a+1)$. Per sum, the dual pairs give $q\approx2.6$ (primitive) and $3.3$ (all), and the non-dual pairs $3.7$ (primitive) and $4.8$ (all). The dual family shows that such fits do not estimate asymptotic exponents. Its primitive pairs are, up to a factor of at most $3$ in the height, points of the del Pezzo surface of Section~\ref{sec:lower}, of which there are $O(y(\log y)^4)$ with height at most $y$ \cite{browningsofos2019}; if, as expected, the positive ones have the same order, the fitted $2.6$ falls well short of the exponent $4$. Its fitted $q$ is not even stable: on $[600,2400]$, $[1200,4800]$ and $[2400,4800]$ it is $2.6$, $2.5$ and $1.8$.

On $[600,4800]$ the two laws are nearly the same function: the log-slope $q/\log s$ of $c(\log s)^q$ equals $\beta$ at $s\approx2100$, inside the window, and they differ only through the curvature of $\log n$ against $\log s$. Over the forward window $4801\le s\le6000$, which the fits do not use, the log-power law exceeds the exact count $\N(6000)-\N(4800)=42\,960$ by $0.25\%$ and the power law by $2.33\%$; with both laws fitted on $[600,3600]$ and compared on $3601\le s\le4800$, the excesses are $0.21\%$ and $2.32\%$. This shows that $\log n$ is concave in $\log s$ on this range, as the falling local exponents of Table~\ref{tab:density} also show, and that a law with this much curvature extrapolates over a short forward window better than a fixed power. It shows nothing about the limiting exponent. The laws $n(s)=cs^{b_0}(\log s)^q$ with $b_0$ fixed and $(c,q)$ fitted, under which $\N(X)$ has the asymptotic exponent $1+b_0$, fit almost as well. For $b_0=0.1$, $0.2$ and $0.3$ the deviance on $[600,4800]$ exceeds that for $b_0=0$ by $1.7$, $4.1$ and $7.1$, against $26\,479$ on $4199$ degrees of freedom, and the excesses in the two forward windows are $0.61\%$ and $0.56\%$, $0.98\%$ and $0.92\%$, and $1.34\%$ and $1.28\%$. The fit worsens only gradually with $b_0$, with no threshold at which a larger $b_0$ is rejected. The free three-parameter law $cs^{\beta}(\log s)^q$, fitted on $[600,4800]$ or on $[600,6000]$, even has $\beta=-0.25$ or $-0.17$, which would make $\N(X)=o(X)$, against Theorem~\ref{thm:A}. So the range of the enumeration is far from any asymptotic regime, and the fitted numbers describe this range only.
