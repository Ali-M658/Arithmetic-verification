summary: 'Garces & Loyola, ''Revisiting a Number-Theoretic Puzzle: The Census-Taker
  Problem'' (Intersection 11 (2010), pp.28-38; arXiv:1204.2071v1 [math.HO], submitted
  10 Apr 2012). Full PDF fetched directly (CLI fetch failed with JUNK_CONTENT on the
  PDF; read via Read tool instead). Defines a census-taker number (CTN) N as a positive
  integer with EXACTLY ONE pair of distinct unordered triples {a,b,c},{d,e,f} of positive
  integers with abc=def=N (equal PRODUCT) AND a+b+c=d+e+f (equal SUM) -- this is the
  classical puzzle where a mother''s product-and-sum clues plus ''oldest daughter''
  uniqueness pin down ages. Smallest CTN is 36, with mysterious triples {9,2,2} and
  {6,6,1}, magic sum 13 (example: also gives 40,72,96 as the four two-digit CTNs).
  Proves: Theorem 1, mysterious triples of a CTN share no common entry; Theorem 2,
  a CTN is never a prime power; Theorem 3, every CTN is a product of >=4 primes. Theorem
  7/Corollary 8: if p and 2p-1 are both prime, N=p^2(2p-1)^2 is a CTN, giving infinitely
  many CTNs conditional on infinitely many such primes (open). States there are 0
  one-digit, 4 two-digit, 29 three-digit, and 277 four-digit CTNs (largest 9996).
  Cites Meyers & See, ''The Census-Taker Problem,'' Mathematics Magazine 63(2) (1990),
  pp.86-88 as the origin of the modern formal treatment, and J.B. Kelly, ''Partitions
  with equal products,'' Proc. AMS 15 (1964), pp.987-990 for a sum-M/equal-product
  dual without the uniqueness condition. CRITICAL DISTINCTION FOR NOVELTY QUESTION:
  this is equal PRODUCT + equal SUM (with a uniqueness/counting condition), structurally
  different from the target paper''s equal SUM + equal SUM-OF-RECIPROCALS problem.
  No reciprocal-sum condition appears anywhere in this paper or its cited literature
  ([1]-[5]); it does not overlap with the {(2,8,8),(3,3,12)} coincidence.'
