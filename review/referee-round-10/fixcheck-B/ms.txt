                                      Abstract
  The Laplace spectrum of a closed orientable hyperbolic 2-orbifold determines its
  signature, the genus and the orders of its cone points, but a measurement or a
  computation yields only finitely many eigenvalues, each to some accuracy. We
  show that this suffices: among orbifolds of area at most A, systole at least ε and
  cone orders at most M , the first N eigenvalues, each known to within δ and none
  omitted, determine the signature, with N and δ given by explicit formulas and
  also computable by a certified procedure that always terminates. The closed-form
  constants are very large; with the gap between signatures computed over the
  finite set of signatures and certified in ball arithmetic, the same argument proves
  that N of order 102 to 104 eigenvalues, each known to within about 6 × 10−4
  to 2.5 × 10−6 , suffice for the classes of our computed examples. The bounds on

      with their first N eigenvalues in a bounded set. Whether the systole bound is
      needed is open. An a-posteriori version of the test, run on ten computed spectra,
      gives consistency checks, not determinations.

1 Introduction
A drum is heard through its tones, and a closed hyperbolic surface or orbifold through
the eigenvalues 0 = λ0 < λ1 ≤ λ2 ≤ · · · of its Laplacian. For a closed orientable
hyperbolic 2-orbifold O, whose singular points are cone points, the whole spectrum
determines the signature σ (O) = (g ; m1 , . . . , mn ), the genus of the underlying surface
and the multiset of cone orders [1, Thm 1.1, Prop. 3.3], and at constant curva-
ture the heat invariants, with the curvature, already determine the cone orders [2,
Cor. 4.21(iv)], through the heat invariants computed in [2, Thm 4.20]; isospectral orb-
ifolds of the same signature need not be isometric [3, Thm A]. An experiment or a
computation, however, delivers finitely many eigenvalues, each with an error. This
paper asks whether such data determine the signature, and with how many eigenvalues
and what accuracy.
    The answer is yes, with explicit numbers, once three quantities are bounded: the
area, from below the systole (the least length of a closed geodesic), and from above
the cone orders. Write Sig for the class of closed orientable hyperbolic 2-orbifolds, and,
for A, ε > 0 and an integer M ≥ 2,

   C (A, ε, M ) = {O ∈ Sig : Area(O) ≤ A, ℓ(O) ≥ ε, all cone orders at most M },

with ℓ(O) the systole; Sig(A, M ) denotes the finite set of hyperbolic signatures of area
in (0, A] with orders at most M ; Theorem 6.2 compares against all of it, not only
against the signatures of the area of O.

    What effectivity adds. Without constants the statement follows from compactness.
Fix two signatures σ ̸= σ ′ in Sig(A, M ). The orbifolds of signature σ with systole at
least ε form a compact subset of moduli space, by Mumford’s theorem [5, Cor. 3], which
Bers extended to Fuchsian groups with elliptic elements [6]; each λj is continuous there
(nearby orbifolds are related by K -bi-Lipschitz orbifold maps, sending cone points to
cone points of the same order, with K near 1; in dimension two such a map changes the
Dirichlet energy by a factor in [K −2 , K 2 ] and the L2 norm by a factor in [K −2 , K 2 ],


                                                   3
so by the min-max principle it changes each λj by a factor in [K −4 , K 4 ]), and two
orbifolds of different signatures have different spectra [1], [7, Thm 1]. Covering the
compact product of the two sets by the open sets where some λj differ gives a finite
N ; for this N the function maxj<N |λj (O) − λj (O′ )| is continuous and positive on
the compact product, so it has a positive minimum, larger than 2δ for some δ > 0;
and Sig(A, M ) is finite. This argument gives no value of N or δ , and no way to check
a given spectrum. Theorem 6.2 gives both numbers as formulas. They are enormous
(Table 6) because Theorem 6.2 bounds the gap between the signature terms of different
signatures through their heat invariants (Lemma 6.1), which is valid only at very small
times, and because it uses worst cases, over the class, of the index and the size of the
first difference of heat invariants (Remark 6.7); with the gap computed over the finite
set Sig(A, M ) at a moderate time and certified in ball arithmetic, the same argument
proves that N of order 102 to 104 eigenvalues, each known to within about 6 × 10−4
to 2.5 × 10−6 , suffice for the classes of our computed examples (Proposition 6.6), and
such a computation always terminates (Theorem 6.5). The formulas of Theorem 6.2
say nothing about the size of the least admissible N , which we do not know.

while Theorem 6.2 asks for 4.32 × 104 to 7.44 × 1012 (rounded up); with a diameter
bound for the instance the counts are 18 to 847 (Table 9). These are consistency
checks on orbifolds whose signature is known, with estimated errors and lists assumed
complete, not determinations of unknown signatures.
    What finite spectral data can detect is limited in three ways that the statements
make explicit. None of the bounds A, ε, M is read off finitely many eigenvalues:
Theorem 1.2 shows that no finite part of the spectrum, known to a fixed accuracy,
bounds the orders, Proposition 8.8 shows the same for the area, and whether one
bounds the systole is Problem 1. They are hypotheses about the class, as one knows in
advance what kind of drum is struck. The accuracies are absolute and, in Theorem 6.2,

far below any measurement. And the data must contain every eigenvalue up to the last
one used, with multiplicity: the sum j<N e−λ̃j t does not depend on the order of the

list, but an omitted eigenvalue λ lowers it by e−λt , which no error budget here covers,
and in our experiments a single omission or duplication can make the test accept a
wrong signature (Section 7).
     The proof runs through the heat trace. By the Selberg trace formula the heat
trace of O is a term Gσ (t) fixed by the signature plus a positive contribution of
the closed geodesics (Theorem 2.1); the small-t expansion of Gσ has coefficients, the
heat invariants, that are integer combinations of explicit rationals. At the first heat
invariant where two signatures differ, the difference is an integer multiple of an explicit
rational number (Lemma 3.3), and with orders at most M that invariant is the M -th
one at the latest (Theorem 3.2). Explicit, enveloping remainders for both expansions
(Proposition 2.3) turn this into a lower bound for |Gσ (t) − Gσ′ (t)| at a small explicit
time; a count of the closed geodesics in terms of A, ε, M alone (Lemma 4.5), from the
separation of the cone points and a packing argument, controls them there, and the
counting function controls the eigenvalues beyond the N -th.
     The three bounds are not all of the same kind. The method uses each of them, the
area through the finiteness of Sig(A, M ) and the count of closed geodesics, the systole
through the closed geodesics; but the bounds on the orders and on the area are also
needed for the statement itself, while for the systole we do not know.

    The cone point of order m sits at the tip of a cone of angle 2π/m and radius
hm , which for large m looks like the end of a cusp, and the eigenvalues below any
fixed level above 41 multiply, as near the bottom 14 of the continuous spectrum of a
cusp; Figure 3 shows the computed eigenvalues. The area bound is needed as well. By
Cheng’s comparison theorem [8, Thm 2.1], the j -th eigenvalue of a closed hyperbolic
surface of diameter d is at most 41 + 4π 2 j 2 /d2 , and closed hyperbolic surfaces of
systole at least ε, finite covers of one such surface, occur in infinitely many genera;
pigeonhole then gives two of different genus whose first N eigenvalues agree to within
δ (Proposition 8.8). Whether the systole bound is needed we do not know (Problem 1).
    Relation to prior work. The closest result is the theorem of Buser and Courtois
[9, (1.1)]: among closed hyperbolic surfaces of genus g ≥ 2 with injectivity radius at
least ε > 0 there is an integer m(g, ε) such that two surfaces with the same first m
eigenvalues have all their eigenvalues the same. Its proof uses compactness and gives no
value of m, and the authors conjecture “that the theorem holds with a constant which
depends only on g ” [9, pp. 523–524]. Theorem 1.1 is an effective counterpart at the
level of the signature, for orbifolds, with approximate eigenvalues; Problem 1 below is
an analogue of their conjecture, in a loose sense. Effectivity is the point: Theorem 6.2


                                              5
turns the compactness argument into a finite procedure that decides the signature from
finitely many approximate eigenvalues with stated accuracy, in principle; for the classes
of Table 6 it asks for up to about 4 × 1030 eigenvalues to accuracies down to 10−277 ,
far beyond any eigenvalue computation, and the usable statement is Proposition 6.6,
with the test of Section 7 as an illustration. Compactness and finiteness of isospectral
sets are classical: an isospectral set of closed hyperbolic surfaces is finite [10], and so
is one of hyperbolic orbisurfaces [11]; isospectral metrics on a surface form a compact
set [12], and isospectral manifolds under geometric bounds fall into finitely many
diffeomorphism types [13]. For orbifolds, the whole spectrum together with a lower
curvature bound allows only finitely many isotropy types and, for isolated singularities,
bounds the number of singular points [14, Main Thms 1–2], and an isospectral family
of 2-orbifolds with a lower curvature bound contains only finitely many diffeomorphism
types, a consequence of the finiteness under lower bounds on curvature and volume
and an upper bound on the diameter, which in the isospectral setting comes from
Stanhope’s diameter bound [14, 15]; Theorem 4.4 gives an explicit diameter bound in
terms of the area, the systole and the orders instead of the spectrum; the orbifolds

For pinched geodesics, small eigenvalues vary continuously and converge to those of
the cusped limit [21], [22, p. 264], and infinitely many eigenvalues accumulate at 41 ,
indeed cluster at every point of [ 14 , ∞) [23, p. 67], [24], [22]; this is the background of
Section 8.3. Computed hyperbolic spectra have been checked against the Selberg trace



                                             6
formula before: Strohmaier and Uski use the heat trace to check the completeness of
computed eigenvalue lists of compact surfaces [25, §7], and Booker, Strömbergsson
and Venkatesh verify computed Maass forms rigorously [26]. The test of Section 7
applies the same idea to test a signature, and our completeness check follows the idea
of Strohmaier and Uski; its tail bound is rigorous only given completeness below the
largest computed eigenvalue, which here is supported, not proved. Finite consistency
conditions also bound spectra in the other direction: linear programming applied to the
Selberg trace formula bounds the systole, the first positive eigenvalue and the number
of small eigenvalues of closed hyperbolic surfaces [27], and bootstrap methods, from the
consistency of integrals of products of eigenfunctions, bound the eigenvalues of closed
hyperbolic manifolds [28] and the spectral gaps of hyperbolic surfaces and 2-orbifolds
[29]. These bound eigenvalues given the topology; here finitely many eigenvalues bound
the topology. Effective rigidity of another kind is known for arithmetic surfaces: the
length sets of two incommensurable ones of bounded area differ at a length bounded
effectively in terms of the area [30]; and for cofinite Fuchsian groups the constant in
the prime geodesic theorem has an effective bound [31], which depends on the group,
while Lemma 4.5 counts closed geodesics, more crudely, in terms of A, ε and M alone.
For the geometric input, Buser’s book bounds the number of closed geodesics of length
at most L on a closed surface of genus g that are not iterates of very short ones
by (g − 1)eL+6 , in terms of the genus alone [32, Lemma 6.6.4]; our count of closed
geodesics (Lemma 4.5) uses instead the separation of the cone points and a packing
argument, and depends on the area, the systole and the orders. The heat expansion of
an orbifold is local [33], [34, Thm 4.8] (see also the erratum [35]), at constant curvature
its coefficients are known in closed form [2, Thm 4.20], and the spectrum determines
the signature [1], [7, Thm 1]; for Euclidean triangles, a triangle that shares its first N
Dirichlet eigenvalues with a given one, N depending on the first two, is congruent to
it [36, Thm 1].

7.1 Numerical methods
Examples. O(2, 8, 8) and O(3, 3, 12) are doubles of hyperbolic triangles; the eight

from the centre to two adjacent sides, sinh a sinh b = 21 and ϑ = log(sinh a/ sinh b).
    Discretisation and solver. The spectrum of the double of P is the union of the
Neumann and Dirichlet spectra of P (the reflection exchanging the copies splits H 1
into even and odd parts); for Oϑ the two mirror symmetries split each further into four
sectors. Each problem −∆E u = λwu, w = 4/(1 − |z|2 )2 , is solved in the Poincaré disc

the geodesic sides represented as rational quadratic splines and the elements curved to
order p, so that the discrete domain is a polynomial approximation of degree p of the
true one (its area agrees to 2 × 10−14 ), with NGSolve [48, 49]; the mass quadrature is

8.2 The area: surfaces of large genus
The bound on the area is needed for the same reason as the bound on the orders,
and here no orbifold is needed: closed hyperbolic surfaces of large genus have large
diameter, so their first eigenvalues are bounded, and those of systole at least ε occur
in infinitely many genera.

Problem 1 (Is a systole bound needed?)S Fix A and M . (a) Is there an N , independent of ε,
such that orbifolds in C(A, 0+ , M ) = ε>0 C(A, ε, M ) with the same first N eigenvalues have
the same signature? (b) Can N and δ > 0 in Theorem 6.2 both be chosen independently of ε?


    Part (a) is a signature-level analogue of the conjecture of Buser and Courtois [9,
pp. 523–524], in a loose sense: the signature is determined by the heat invariants, the
smooth part of the spectrum, while their conjecture concerns full isospectrality, which
the closed geodesics carry. With A and M fixed only finitely many signatures occur,
so the pigeonhole argument of Proposition 8.3 is not available. Under pinching, as
recalled in Section 1, the small eigenvalues converge to those of the cusped limit and
infinitely many eigenvalues accumulate at 14 . So a negative answer to (b) would follow
from two degenerating families of different signatures but the same area, with the same
number of eigenvalues tending to 0 and cusped limits with the same eigenvalues below
1
4 , while (a) asks for exact equality and is not settled by such limits. Signatures of the
same area degenerate differently: (0; 3, 3, 3, 3), (1; 3) and (0; 2, 2, 6, 6) all have area 4π
                                                                                             3 ,
but on (1; 3) every essential simple closed curve is non-separating, since a disc with one
cone point of order 3 cannot have geodesic boundary (its area would be −2π· 13 < 0), so
its pinchings have limit (0; 3, ∞, ∞), and we expect λ1 to stay away from 0 (we do not


                                                38
prove it; it would need the orbifold version of the convergence results discussed next),
while (0; 3, 3, 3, 3) pinched along a separating curve has λ1 → 0 (Proposition 8.9).
In our setting: the upper bounds above give lim sup λj ≤ 14 , and the missing input
is a matching lower bound λj ≥ 41 − o(1) for 2 ≤ j < N along both families. The
natural candidate pair for a negative answer to (b) is (0; 3, 3, 3, 3) and (0; 2, 2, 6, 6):
pinched along a separating curve, with the partition {3, 3}|{3, 3} and {2, 6}|{2, 6} (a
disc with cone points of orders 2, 2 and geodesic boundary would have area 0), each
has an eigenvalue tending to 0 (by a test function equal to ±1 on the two sides of
the collar, as in Proposition 8.9), and the limits are two copies of (0; 3, 3, ∞) and two
copies of (0; 2, 6, ∞); what this pair needs is the spectrum of these two cusped limits
below 14 and the lower bound discussed next. For surfaces both available routes to such
a bound are theorems: under degeneration the spectral counting functions converge
below 14 [52], a closed hyperbolic surface of Euler characteristic χ has λ−χ > 14 [53],
and Ballmann, Matthiesen and Mondal bound the number of small eigenvalues by −χ
for complete surfaces of finite type [54]. What is missing here is an orbifold version
of either: the first for the lift of the degeneration to a fixed torsion-free cover, whose
spectrum contains that of the orbifold, together with the spectrum below 14 of the
cusped limit; or the second for cone metrics with angles 2π/k . We have not carried
out either. The growth of our N as ε → 0 (Section 6) is a property of the formulas,
not of the least admissible N .
