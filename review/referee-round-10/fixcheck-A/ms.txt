1 Introduction
Put a unit of heat at a point of a closed surface and let it spread. For a short time t
the heat kernel on the diagonal depends, up to an error smaller than every power of t,
only on the geometry in a fixed neighbourhood of the point  √ (Kac’s principle), and in
that neighbourhood it is governed by distances of order t. On a closed hyperbolic 2-
orbifold O a smooth point looks like every other smooth point, because the curvature
is −1 everywhere. A cone point of order m looks like a disc folded m times around its
centre: in an orbifold chart the j -th image of a point at distance r from the cone point
lies at distance about 2r sin(πj/m) (exactly so in the flat approximation; at curvature
−1, sinh(d/2) = sinh r sin(πj/m)), so   √ the cone point raises the heat kernel on the
diagonal
      √    out to a distance of about    t/ sin(π/m), which for large m is much larger
than t (Figure 1). The trace of the heat kernel adds up these local contributions; it
has an asymptotic expansion

    This paper asks how many coefficients of this expansion are needed before it dis-
tinguishes the cone points of one orbifold from those of another, and what decides
that number. If two orbifolds share c1 , . . . , ck but not ck+1 , the difference of their heat
traces has exact order tk−1 ; so the least number of heat invariants that determines the
genus and the cone orders counts the orders in t at which cone points of different orders
contribute identically to the expansion. It counts coefficients of an asymptotic expan-
sion, not lengths of time: on any interval of positive times the heat trace determines
the whole spectrum, and with it every heat invariant, and the companion manuscript
[3] decides the signature from finitely many eigenvalues through values of the heat
trace at positive times. Short-time diffusion therefore detects the signature through
these coefficients only in the limit t → 0: Lemma B.5 and Remark B.6 recover c3 of
O(2, 8, 8) from values of the heat trace with a proved error, but in our scans only
from sets of times reaching below 10−3 , with values accurate to a few times 10−8 or
better, while the computed spectra of this paper fix the trace only for t ≳ 10−3 . The
counts below are invariants of the sequence of coefficients, not durations of an exper-
iment. Geometrically, a count measures how many orders of the short-time expansion
pass before cone points of different orders stop contributing alike; Proposition 2.17
shows that this is a statement about constant curvature, where the smooth part of
every invariant is fixed by the area. Two scales limit what the expansion describes. A
cone point of order m enters through a function with poles at distance π/m from the
origin (Section 2.2); its coefficients grow like l−1/2 l! (m/π )2l (Lemma B.3), and the

1.1 Main results
Theorem 1.1 (Bounded cone orders) Let M ≥ 2.
 (i) Among orbifolds in Sig whose cone orders are all at most M , the first M heat invariants
     determine the genus and the cone orders, and the first M − 1 do not.
(ii) Against all of Sig, the first M + 1 heat invariants determine the genus and the cone
     orders of an orbifold whose cone orders are at most M , at every area, and M + 1 cannot
     be lowered uniformly in the area: for every M some pair, of large enough area, shares
     exactly c1 , . . . , cM ; for M = 2, (0; 2, . . . , 2) with ten cone points and (1; 4, 4, 4, 4), both
     of area 6π, share exactly c1 , c2 .
(iii) So the growth in Theorem 1.2 below is carried by large cone orders: an orbifold that

Theorem 1.4 (Triangle orbifolds) Let O(p, q, r) be a hyperbolic triangle orbifold.
 (i) The first four heat invariants determine its signature among all of Sig (Corollary 5.3);
     whether three do is equivalent to the absence of solutions of one Diophantine system
     (Problem 5). Among hyperbolic triangle orbifolds the first three heat invariants determine
     O(p, q, r) up to isometry, and if p + q + r ≤ 17 the first two do.
(ii) The orbifolds O(2, 8, 8) and O(3, 3, 12) share their first two heat invariants; among
     hyperbolic triangle orbifolds they are the only such pair with cone-order sum 18, and no
     pair has a smaller sum.
(iii) This pair is isolated among triangle orbifolds: for every integer k ≥ 1 the orbifolds
      O(2k, 8k, 8k) and O(3k, 3k, 12k) share their first two heat invariants with each other

      points on a plane cubic, which here is an elliptic curve of rank 0 with twelve rational
      points. For k = 1 the pair is isolated in all of Sig (Theorem 5.10); for k ≥ 2 we claim
      isolation among triangle orbifolds only.

    Section 6 concerns the algebraic map that recovers the cone orders of a sphere with
n cone points from c1 , . . . , cn , extended to real orders, where the cj are an algebraic
device and not heat invariants of an orbifold. This map is Lipschitz at simple orders
and Hölder of exponent 1/k at k -fold orders, with constants explicit except for one
matrix norm, which is itself bounded explicitly (Appendix C); the exponent 1/k cannot
be improved for arbitrary data, and for data that are the cj of real orders it is 12 at
a double order and when all n ≥ 3 orders are equal, while other configurations are
open. Its content for orbifolds is the threshold below which integer orders are recovered
exactly (Theorem C.4, Proposition C.5, Corollary 6.5). Approximate c1 , . . . , cn can be
obtained from finitely many values of the heat trace with a proved error (Lemma B.5),
but for the minimal pair this reaches the threshold only from sets of times reaching
below 10−3 (Remark B.6), shorter than computed spectra resolve.
    What is analytic. The analytic results are these. Proposition 2.8, proved in
Appendix B from the trace formula alone, gives the full small-time expansion of the
heat trace of a hyperbolic orbifold in closed form, with explicit remainders valid at
every t > 0: the area series and the cone series have enveloping remainders of opposite
signs (Proposition B.2, an elementary consequence of the sign of the Taylor remainder
of e−x ), which give the bound (6). The cone series has the scale (π/m)2 : its coeffi-
cients grow like l−1/2 l! (m/π )2l (Lemma B.3, Darboux’s method at the nearest poles),
and truncated at the optimal order it leaves an error exponentially small on that scale
(Proposition B.4). With a bound for the closed geodesics in terms of the systole and
the diameter (Lemma 2.5), these give heat invariants from finitely many values of the
heat trace with a proved error (Lemma B.5). In variable curvature, Theorem 1.6(i)
holds at every order; beyond order t2 its proof rests on a parametric form of Donnelly’s
expansion (Lemma E.1) and on the identification of the derivatives of the localised

    What is the algebra of odd power sums. The other input is the triangular structure
of the heat invariants (Lemma 2.11), which is Uçar’s: each new coefficient adds one
new odd power sum of the cone orders. From there on the counts are statements
about odd power sums and the reciprocal sum of a signed multiset of orders, and
much of it is classical. Corollary 1.5 and the first sentence of Theorem 1.3(i) follow
from Newton’s identities and Theorem 1.1(i) from a Vandermonde determinant; the
separation theorem behind them (Theorem 3.7) is the analogue, for odd power sums
with the reciprocal sum, of the classical bound N (k ) ≥ k + 1, and is proved in the
same way. The new results on this side are the exact area-free answer M + 1 of
Theorem 3.11(ii)–(iv), obtained with the classical sign-change property of Chebyshev
systems [6] (Lemma 3.10) and with witnesses that are divided differences at the nodes
a2 [7], together with its geometric reading, that the growth of the count is carried by
large cone orders (Corollary 3.12); the price of a change of genus in Theorem 1.3(ii),
by Descartes’ rule of signs for polynomials with few nonzero coefficients [8, Part V];
the closed form of the determinant of the linear system in Theorem 1.3(i), which
vanishes exactly where a Hurwitz determinant does; the least configurations and their
relation to the Prouhet–Tarry–Escott problem (Lemma A.1, Proposition 3.19); and,
for triangle orbifolds, Theorem 1.4, whose main geometric statement is the bound of
four invariants against all of Sig and whose arithmetic part is (iii). Theorem 1.2 is a
reformulation: it makes linear growth and power lower bounds equivalent to an open
problem of Diophantine analysis, through the least size of a configuration of signed
cone orders with vanishing low odd moments (Proposition 3.19), without settling it.
    Collisions are not isospectral. Orbifolds in Sig with different signatures have dif-
ferent spectra: by [9, Thm 1.1] the spectrum determines the number of cone points of
each order, and by [9, Prop. 3.3] the underlying surface, which together give the sig-
nature; Doyle and Rossetti proved this by a different method [10, Thm 1]. Isospectral
non-isometric pairs exist in Sig: those of [11, Thm A] have signature (0; 2, 2, 2, 2, 2, 3, 4)
[11, the sentence following Thm A]; they share a signature and hence every heat invari-
ant (Section 4). So the collisions below are statements about short-time diffusion, not
about spectral rigidity.

1.2 Prior work and what is new
What is new, in brief. (a) Analytic: the linear part of the cone terms in variable
curvature at every order (Proposition 2.14, with Lemmas E.1 and E.2) and the exten-
sion and obstruction results of Proposition 2.17; the optimal truncation of the cone
series (Proposition B.4, with Lemma B.3) and the proved extraction of heat invariants
from finitely many values of the heat trace (Lemma B.5); and the explicit constant of
Proposition 4.2(a). The signed remainders of Proposition B.2 are elementary, and the
second-variation constants ωn of Proposition 2.17(ii) are re-derived, in the form we
need, from a classical computation (Remark 2.18). (b) Power sums: the exact answer
M +1 for bounded orders (Theorem 3.11(ii)–(iv)) and its consequence that the growth
is carried by large cone orders (Corollary 3.12), the Descartes bound (Theorem 3.15),
and the reduction of the growth to the Prouhet–Tarry–Escott problem (Theorem 3.18,
Proposition 3.19). (c) Triangle orbifolds and arithmetic: the four invariants against
all of Sig (Corollary 5.3), the main geometric result of Section 5, the least-area state-
ment of Theorem 5.10, and the isolation of the minimal pair among triangle orbifolds
(Theorem 5.11). Theorem 1.2 settles no growth exponent; it makes each exponent
equivalent to a statement about the Prouhet–Tarry–Escott problem.

    Power sums. By Newton’s identities, two n-multisets of complex numbers with
equal P1 , P3 , . . . , P2n−1 agree after deleting pairs {a, −a} (Lemma 3.1 with the argu-
ment of Theorem 3.2; odd symmetric systems are studied in [5, §3]), and for n distinct
positive orders the conclusion of Theorem 3.2 follows from a uniqueness theorem that
Laurens attributes to Steinig and states right after the statement of his Lemma 3.2:
any n power sums of n distinct positive real numbers have at most one solution [33,
after the statement of Lemma 3.2]; see also [34, Prop. 24]. Theorem 3.2 adds com-
plex multisets with multiplicity, for the system (R, P1 , . . . , P2n−3 ) that heat supplies,
and Theorem 3.3 the closed form of the determinant; the linear system is the ana-
logue, with R, of the odd-power-sum Newton identities of [35, §3]. By our search
(Remark 3.5), the integer witness of Theorem 3.4(3) and the seven other primitive
witnesses with orders at most 84 are exactly (A.685)–(A.692) of Chen’s survey, which

3 Hearing the signature
Comparing two orbifolds means studying one signed multiset, the cone orders of the
first together with the negatives of those of the second, whose reciprocal sum and
low odd moments vanish. Most of what follows comes from one fact: such a multiset,
if small compared with the number of vanishing moments, is symmetric under x 7→
−x; the bounds on the cone orders and on the genus rest instead on sign changes
(Lemma 3.10, Theorem 3.15).

Heat invariants are local: up to an error smaller than every power of t, the heat trace is

7 Open problems
By Proposition 3.19 the growth questions are questions about the minimal configura-
tion size T (L).

Problem 1 (Linear growth) Is T (L) = O(L), that is, N (k) = O(k), that is, f (A) = Θ(A)?


Problem 2 (Ideal configurations) Is T (L) = 2L + 2 for every L? It holds for L = 2, 3; the
first open case is T (4) ∈ [10, 14].


Problem 3 (The first genus collision) Is Tg (3) = 8 or 10 (Remark 3.22; the exclusion there
is computer-assisted)?


Problem 4 (Integer sharpness) For n ≥ 5, do hyperbolic spheres with n cone points and
different orders share n−1 heat invariants? By Theorem 3.4(1) this asks for an odd symmetric
Prouhet–Tarry–Escott system [5, §3] of n positive and n negative integers with vanishing
reciprocal sum, that is, for a balanced ideal (n − 1)-configuration, the balanced case of T (n −
1) = 2n (Problem 2); yes for n = 3, 4, and over the reals. In the notation of [36] it asks for
an ideal solution of type (−1, 1, 3, . . . , 2n − 5).
